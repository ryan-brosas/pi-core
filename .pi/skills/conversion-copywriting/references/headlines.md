# Conversion Page Headlines

## Job

Help an agent create and evaluate headlines for pages that ask a defined audience to take a commercial action. The headline's immediate task is to earn relevant attention and establish a useful first connection. It should make the rest of the page worth reading, not attempt to complete the entire sale by itself.

This reference concerns conversion-page headlines, not editorial or blog titles.

## Inputs

Collect or explicitly mark as unknown:

- Page type and primary conversion goal
- Traffic source, query, ad, email, or preceding step
- Intended reader or segment
- Reader's likely problem, desired outcome, and stage of awareness
- Voice-of-customer language from interviews, surveys, reviews, support, chat, or search terms
- Offer, differentiator, product behavior, and strongest defensible benefit
- Available proof for claims, quantities, timing, and comparisons
- Brand voice and topic sensitivity
- Current headline or a plain placeholder, subhead, CTA, baseline behavior, and test capacity
- A tagged swipe library with source, date, context, and known or unknown performance status
- Legal, ethical, space, accessibility, device, and design constraints

## Decision Rules

1. **Match the arrival context.** Reflect the motivation and expectations created by the source. Repeating a keyword mechanically is not required; preserving the visitor's intent is.
2. **Lead with reader value.** Prefer the problem resolved, progress enabled, or outcome sought over a self-description of the company.
3. **Match the lead to awareness.** A problem-aware reader usually needs the problem or desired progress recognized. A solution-aware reader needs the approach connected to the outcome. A product-aware reader may need the product, fit, or distinction. A ready reader may respond to the offer, proof, terms, or a legitimate incentive.
4. **Protect comprehension first.** A reader should be able to tell whether the page is relevant and why continuing may help. Personality may strengthen a clear message, but it must not carry essential meaning alone.
5. **Keep one reader, one main idea, one promise, and one offer in view.** Do not use a headline to reconcile several audiences or unrelated value propositions.
6. **Use formulas and swipes as drafting scaffolds.** Abstract a useful structure or persuasion move, then write new language for the present reader, product, market, device, and era. Never copy the source line or assume its reported performance will transfer.
7. **Separate generation from judgment.** During an ideation round, produce candidates without polishing or rejecting each line. Shortlist and edit only after the round so early judgment does not collapse the range of options.
8. **Generate across message angles, not just wording variants.** Explore audience, outcome, problem, mechanism, differentiation, speed, proof, and contrast where the inputs support them. A headline is often a compressed value proposition.
9. **Be specific only to the level supported.** Numbers, timeframes, popularity, exclusivity, and comparisons need current evidence. Never manufacture precision.
10. **Keep curiosity honest.** The page must promptly satisfy the expectation the headline creates. Reject bait-and-switch, exaggerated threat, and unsupported emotional pressure.
11. **Fit voice to audience and stakes.** Humor or unusual syntax may aid recall when the reader still understands the offer and the topic permits it. Do not force the writer's personality into sensitive or high-risk situations.
12. **Use the subhead deliberately.** It can clarify audience, mechanism, proof, or qualification, but should not rescue a headline that is incomprehensible on its own.
13. **Evaluate the headline with its neighbors.** The hero image, subhead, proof, offer, and CTA form one treatment. A weak or generic button can suppress the value of a strong headline, and the reverse is also true.
14. **Do not impose an arbitrary word or candidate count.** Remove words that do not help understanding, relevance, value, or voice. Generate enough distinct options to escape the first obvious idea, but do not confuse quantity with quality.
15. **Treat precedent as a hypothesis.** Historical case results and swipe claims show what may have worked for another audience and context. They do not establish a universal winning pattern.
16. **Treat timed drills as practice, not validation.** Speed can build fluency and reduce self-editing, but an unfinished sprint output is not ready for publication until it passes evidence, clarity, context, and tone review.

## Workflow

1. **Write the headline brief.** In one short block, state who arrived, from where, what they want, what they know, what action the page requests, and why the offer is credible.
2. **Understand the product and outcome.** Review the relevant page, offer, features, use cases, and proof until the benefit and its significance to the reader can be stated without guessing. If there is no control, write a plain placeholder that captures the current best-supported message.
3. **Extract source language.** Build a small bank of recurring customer terms for pains, desired outcomes, alternatives, objections, and moments of urgency. Preserve meaning without blindly copying awkward phrasing.
4. **Define the message territory.** List the strongest supported benefit, differentiator, proof point, problem, mechanism, awareness-matched lead, and offer. Exclude claims the evidence cannot sustain.
5. **Select structural prompts.** Review tagged swipes and formulas for useful structures, not reusable prose. Note whether performance is documented, anecdotal, or unknown, and prefer variety over repeated versions of one pattern.
6. **Draft in separate rounds.** Use several formula or swipe structures to produce rough candidates quickly without editing them in place. Then draft additional candidates without prompts so the set does not converge on one cadence.
7. **Vary the value proposition.** Group candidates by underlying promise. If a group contains only synonyms, add a genuinely different supported angle.
8. **Shortlist after generation.** Remove candidates that rest on guessed product value, mismatched awareness, unsupported claims, or copied surface language. Retain a small set of strategically distinct options before polishing.
9. **Pair candidates with subheads and CTAs.** Check whether each three-part sequence forms a coherent path from relevance to value to action.
10. **Score comparatively.** Use a consistent scale for:
   - Arrival-context and awareness match
   - Attention without manipulation
   - Plain-language comprehension
   - Reader-centered relevance
   - Defensible benefit or value
   - Differentiation and memorability, after clarity
11. **Reject before final polishing.** Remove lines that are vague, generic, brand-centered, unsupported, tonally unsafe, or dependent on missing context below them.
12. **Edit the finalists.** Tighten syntax, replace abstractions with concrete language, remove jargon, and read aloud. Preserve the customer's meaning and the brand's appropriate voice.
13. **Validate in context.** Review the rendered hero at target breakpoints, devices, and with the actual traffic message. Use comprehension testing, preference evidence only as secondary input, and an experiment when traffic and consequences justify one.
14. **Record learning.** Document the audience, source, awareness hypothesis, complete treatment, structural inspiration, dates, metric, result quality, anomalies, and next question. Do not record only the winning sentence.

## Controlled Failures

- **Missing audience or traffic context:** Do not invent specificity. Return a provisional plain-language headline and list the minimum research needed.
- **Insufficient product understanding:** Stop the sprint, identify the unresolved benefit or desired outcome, and gather product and customer evidence. Do not fill a formula with guessed value.
- **Mixed-intent traffic:** Recommend segmentation or choose the highest-priority convertible segment explicitly. Do not claim to address everyone equally.
- **Unsupported outcome, quantity, or timeframe:** Remove it, qualify it accurately, or request evidence.
- **Clever line fails a quick comprehension check:** Keep any useful emotional angle, then rewrite it plainly or move personality into secondary copy.
- **Formula or swipe output sounds copied, canned, or manipulative:** Preserve only the underlying strategic angle or structure and rewrite from the current evidence.
- **Swipe performance is unknown:** Label it as structural inspiration, not proof, and judge the resulting treatment in the current context.
- **Sensitive topic conflicts with playful brand voice:** Favor reader safety, dignity, and clarity.
- **Headline and CTA tell different stories:** Revise them as a pair before testing either one.
- **Timed practice produces weak or incomplete lines:** Keep them as rough material, not finalists. Resume with research, shortlisting, and deliberate editing.
- **No viable A/B test:** Use message matching, moderated comprehension checks, five-second recall cautiously, and post-launch behavioral evidence. Label conclusions as provisional.
- **Inconclusive experiment:** Keep the control, inspect instrumentation and segment quality, and form a new hypothesis. Do not declare equivalence or a general rule.
- **A historical example reports a lift:** Treat it as case-specific evidence only; never project that figure onto the current page.

## Validation

Before delivery, confirm that:

- The headline identifies a relevant promise, problem, product, offer, or outcome for the intended reader's awareness level.
- A reader can understand the core meaning without insider knowledge.
- One reader, one governing idea, one promise, and one offer remain coherent across the hero.
- The line matches the ad, query, email, or route that precedes it.
- The writer understood the product and desired outcome rather than filling gaps with invention.
- Every factual, comparative, numeric, or time-bound claim has evidence.
- The page fulfills any curiosity or expectation the headline creates.
- Swipe or formula influence is structural only; the final language is original and current-context appropriate.
- The tone is suitable for the audience and subject.
- The subhead adds useful information rather than repairing confusion.
- The headline, hero, proof, and CTA support one coherent action.
- Mobile wrapping, line breaks, hierarchy, and accessible reading order preserve meaning.
- Any recommendation based on past test results is framed as a hypothesis.
- The validation method measures the intended outcome, not merely stakeholder taste or writing speed.
