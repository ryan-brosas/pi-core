# Conversion-Focused Web Pages

## Job

Help an agent research, structure, write, wireframe, and validate a web page whose words should move a defined reader toward a defined action. Build a supported argument rather than a catalog of facts. Match the amount and order of copy to what the visitor already knows and what they still need to believe.

## Inputs

Collect or mark as unknown:

- Page type: home, campaign landing, product, service, sales, pricing, or another conversion page
- Primary action and business outcome
- Traffic sources and message immediately before the visit
- Priority audience or segment
- Visitor stage of awareness and likely intent
- Customer language from interviews, surveys, reviews, support, chat, and search behavior
- Product, offer, differentiators, benefits, drawbacks, alternatives, and objections
- Direct product access or a documented walkthrough of the full customer experience
- For pricing pages, the plan model, billing basis, usage rules, commercially preferred outcome, and reason each option exists
- Relevant value details beyond features, such as the product's development, expertise, process, materials, operating history, or other supportable facts
- Proof assets and permission to use them
- Existing page, analytics, scroll/click evidence, user recordings, and baseline metrics
- Navigation, design-system, technical, legal, accessibility, and brand constraints
- Testing capacity and the metric that reflects the requested outcome

## Decision Rules

1. **Classify the page before writing.** A campaign landing page usually receives controlled traffic and should pursue one action. A home page receives broader traffic and may route visitors, but it still needs a clear priority and a primary audience.
2. **Define the action first.** Work backward from what the visitor must do. Include only content that establishes relevance, increases value, supplies proof, answers an objection, reduces risk, or makes that action easier.
3. **Write for one priority reader.** A page may be visible to many people, but undifferentiated copy usually weakens the case. Segment traffic or choose the most valuable plausible reader.
4. **Match message to awareness.** Visitors who already know the product may need the offer and risk details. Visitors who know only the problem need a longer bridge through solution and product understanding.
5. **Let the argument determine length.** Do not target “short” or “long.” Use enough copy to move the intended visitor from their current belief state to a justified next step, then remove repetition and tangents.
6. **Use research to set hierarchy.** Recurrence, intensity, specificity, segment fit, and business relevance should determine what appears first. Internal preference is not a substitute for customer evidence.
7. **Lead with benefits but explain the mechanism.** State the progress or relief the reader values, then make clear how the product enables it. Features matter when they make a benefit believable or answer a buying question.
8. **Make claims proportionate to proof.** Place relevant evidence near the claim or objection it supports. Choose testimonials and case material that match the audience, offer, and page goal.
9. **Preserve narrative transitions.** Each section should answer the question raised by the previous one. Crossheads, short connective phrases, layout, and ordering should let scanners recover the logic.
10. **Use problem-led frameworks selectively.** A problem-first sequence can suit problem-aware readers when research shows the pain is salient and ethically discussable. It is not a default for every home page or audience.
11. **Limit exits on focused landing pages.** Remove or mute unrelated navigation and secondary links when the page has one campaign goal. Retain required legal, privacy, accessibility, and trust paths.
12. **Keep repeated CTAs consistent.** A long page may repeat the same action at moments of readiness. Repetition does not create multiple goals if every primary control leads to the same outcome.
13. **Treat design and copy as one system.** Wireframe the message hierarchy. Do not allow decoration, columns, or visual reordering to break the argument or hide proof.
14. **Treat case studies as local evidence.** A result from one historical page supports a new test idea, not a universal design or copy rule.
15. **Treat the pricing area as persuasive content.** Do not reduce it to a placeholder between a headline and FAQs. Plan names, order, distinctions, emphasis, calculations, and supporting copy all affect whether readers can identify a fit.
16. **Investigate what a price objection means.** A price complaint may reflect a true budget limit, weak perceived value, unclear fit, switching burden, or uncertainty about results. Research the underlying barrier before discounting or changing presentation.
17. **Do comparison work for the reader.** When savings, replacement costs, usage economics, or plan differences matter, show transparent calculations with assumptions and the full billing basis. Do not expect visitors to derive the comparison themselves.
18. **Treat pricing architecture as a hypothesis.** Option count, plan order, visual contrast, anchoring, and recommended-plan emphasis may shape comparison, but no arrangement is universally best. Keep every option real, legible, and defensible, then test against completed purchases, plan mix, average order value, refunds, and retention.
19. **Reveal supportable sources of value.** Relevant facts about how the product was made, who developed it, what expertise informed it, or how it has matured can strengthen the value case. Include only details that matter to the buyer and can be verified.
20. **Keep cost disclosure clear.** A useful per-day, per-seat, or per-use comparison may aid understanding, but it must sit beside the actual charge, billing period, renewal terms, and assumptions. Never make price hard to notice or decode.

## Workflow

1. **Write a page brief.** State page type, traffic source, priority reader, awareness, desired action, business metric, offer, and constraints.
2. **Audit the current experience.** Record a structured walkthrough. Note unanswered questions, competing goals, unclear claims, weak transitions, dead zones, and evidence gaps without editing yet.
3. **Experience and document the product.** Use the product or review a complete demonstration when access is possible. Record setup, use, delivery, support, and the factual details behind its value. Separate observed facts from company assertions.
4. **Research the audience and behavior.** Use segmented customer surveys, interviews with new/current/former customers, reviews, chat logs, search terms, analytics, click/scroll maps, and observed usability sessions as available. Probe past price hesitation beyond the first answer to distinguish affordability from value, fit, and risk concerns.
5. **Synthesize a research summary.** Produce:
   - Priority audience and context
   - Jobs, pains, desired outcomes, and alternatives
   - Objections and anxieties
   - Recurring customer language
   - Feature-to-benefit mappings
   - Proof inventory
   - Proposed messaging hierarchy
   - Open questions and confidence levels
6. **Set the page's argument.** Write the core proposition, then outline the beliefs required before action. Map each belief to a section and a suitable proof type.
7. **Choose a sequence.** Common functions include relevance, problem or desired state, solution category, product mechanism, differentiated value, proof, objection handling, offer, risk reduction, and close. Reorder or omit functions based on awareness and evidence.
8. **Draft from the action backward.** Define the CTA and the value on its far side. Draft the close, objection handling, proof, body argument, and hero so each part prepares the next decision.
9. **Design pricing content deliberately when present.** Map each plan to a real job or usage pattern, state its complete cost and constraints, calculate meaningful comparisons, choose an initial order and emphasis with a written rationale, and place proof or risk reduction beside the doubts it addresses.
10. **Wireframe while writing.** Show headings, copy blocks, proof, media purpose, CTA placement, required links, and intended transitions. Label imagery by communicative job rather than appearance alone.
11. **Edit in passes.** Check first for argument and missing evidence, then relevance and order, then clarity and specificity, then concision, voice, and mechanics.
12. **Review with design and implementation.** Confirm the visual hierarchy, responsive order, forms, links, pricing calculations, and interactions preserve the copy's logic and no-JavaScript or fallback behavior where required.
13. **Validate before and after launch.** Use message-match review, comprehension sessions, usability testing, click/scroll evidence, form analytics, and controlled experiments when feasible. For pricing treatments, measure paid outcomes and downstream quality rather than plan-card clicks alone.
14. **Document outcomes.** Record the full treatment, audience, source, hypothesis, metric, duration, result quality, anomalies, and follow-up question.

## Controlled Failures

- **Unknown page goal:** Stop drafting and request a decision. If work must continue, label one provisional primary action and quarantine secondary requests.
- **Unknown or mixed audience:** Recommend segmented pages or choose a priority segment with an explicit assumption. Do not flatten conflicting needs into generic copy.
- **No customer evidence:** Produce a low-claim structural draft and a research plan. Do not fabricate voice-of-customer language.
- **No product access or reliable walkthrough:** Mark mechanism and experience claims as unverified. Request access or limit the draft to documented facts.
- **Unclear awareness level:** Infer cautiously from traffic source and behavior, provide alternative sequences, and identify what would resolve the uncertainty.
- **Proof missing for a claim:** Weaken, qualify, relocate, or remove the claim. Never use decorative proof that does not support the nearby message.
- **Stakeholders require several equal goals on a landing page:** Surface the conflict, recommend a hierarchy or separate pages, and show the likely distraction path.
- **Page feels too long:** Audit the argument for repetition and irrelevant branches. Do not cut sections solely because they fall below the fold.
- **Page feels too short:** Identify unanswered beliefs, objections, mechanisms, or proof. Do not add filler to reach a target length.
- **Problem-led copy risks shame or distress:** Use neutral recognition, outcomes, or solution-aware framing instead. Do not intensify pain beyond what research and ethics support.
- **A price objection has no diagnosed cause:** Do not assume a discount or visual treatment will solve it. Gather decision evidence or present the proposed change as a narrow test.
- **A preferred plan has no reader-fit rationale:** Remove the recommendation treatment or state the actual basis without implying superiority.
- **A price comparison depends on hidden assumptions:** Show the assumptions and full charge, or remove the comparison.
- **A proposed layout obscures cost:** Reject the treatment. Price hierarchy may be restrained, but cost, billing, renewal, and limits must remain easy to find and understand.
- **Research methods disagree:** Preserve segment and method differences, lower confidence, and test the disputed message rather than averaging it away.
- **No experiment is viable:** Use qualitative comprehension and usability evidence plus post-launch behavioral monitoring. Mark causal claims as unavailable.
- **A prior case reports a conversion change:** Cite it only as historical context; do not promise or forecast the same effect.

## Validation

Before delivery, confirm that:

- The page type, traffic source, priority reader, and primary action are explicit.
- The hero matches the visitor's arrival context and awareness.
- Every section has a defined role in the argument.
- The sequence moves from what the visitor knows toward the requested action without logical gaps.
- Copy length follows the persuasion burden, not a template or fold rule.
- Benefits, mechanisms, objections, and alternatives are expressed in customer-relevant language.
- Material claims are supported, qualified, and permitted for public use.
- Testimonials and proof match the segment and the offer.
- Focused landing pages do not contain avoidable competing paths.
- Repeated CTAs name and perform the same action.
- The wireframe preserves reading order, transitions, mobile scanning, accessible headings, labels, and controls.
- Forms explain what happens next and request only necessary information.
- Pricing options map to real jobs or usage patterns and make differences easy to compare.
- Any savings, replacement-cost, per-unit, or per-period calculation is accurate, shows its assumptions, and sits beside the full charge and billing terms.
- Plan order, visual emphasis, and recommendation badges have documented rationales and do not conceal viable options.
- Pricing experiments use completed and downstream outcomes, including plan mix or average order value when relevant, rather than attention alone.
- The chosen metric matches the business question; clicks alone are not presented as completed sales or retained customers.
- Historical results are not generalized into guaranteed outcomes.
