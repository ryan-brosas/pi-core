# Research Reference

## Job

Build an evidence base for copy and messaging decisions. The agent should discover how a defined audience describes its situation, alternatives, decision, concerns, and desired progress, then turn those observations into a ranked set of message hypotheses. Research informs copy; it does not guarantee performance.

## Inputs

- Business objective, page or campaign goal, and intended decision
- Clearly bounded audience or customer segment
- Offer details, product behavior, origin, delivery, constraints, price, commitments, use cases, and known alternatives
- Existing customers, former customers, prospects, subscribers, or site visitors
- Interview recordings and transcripts, sales or demo recordings, survey responses, support conversations, reviews, forum discussions, and post-action polls
- Analytics, click maps, and scroll maps
- Competitor pages and category conventions
- Prior tests, current baseline, and known evidence gaps
- Consent, recording, privacy, attribution, and retention requirements

## Decision Rules

1. **Choose evidence for the question.** Use interviews for sequence, context, motives, emotion, and follow-up depth. Use surveys to check how broadly a pattern may occur. Use reviews or public discussions when direct access is limited. Use analytics and interaction maps to observe behavior, not infer motive. Use competitor audits to understand category claims and alternatives, not to imitate wording.
2. **Triangulate.** Do not let one technique, one vivid quotation, or one stakeholder opinion determine the strategy. Seek agreement or useful tension across independent sources.
3. **Research the audience's desired progress, not merely the product category.** For review mining, look at products, services, communities, or resources people use to reach the same outcome, including indirect alternatives and doing nothing.
4. **Prefer remembered events over hypothetical preferences.** Ask about a recent search, purchase, switch, or use episode. Reconstruct the sequence from the first disruption through evaluation, choice, use, and result.
5. **Pursue concrete detail.** Clarify who was present, what happened next, what had already been tried, what changed, and why a detail mattered. Treat broad labels such as “easy” or “better” as prompts for follow-up, not finished findings.
6. **Keep questions neutral.** Avoid praise, blame, presumed feelings, forced binaries, and embedded conclusions. In surveys, include a genuine alternative and a non-response option where appropriate.
7. **Separate observation from interpretation.** Preserve source wording and provenance in research notes. Put coding, summaries, and agent inferences in distinct fields.
8. **Count recurrence without erasing intensity or context.** Repeated language is a useful signal, but rare high-stakes objections and segment-specific needs may still deserve attention.
9. **Use fast retrieval as triage, not as a substitute for research.** Site-limited searches and recurring phrase markers can surface likely review passages quickly. Search snippets are locators only. Open the source, inspect the surrounding review, confirm segment fit, and retain provenance before using a finding.
10. **Prefer active review when delivery signals matter.** Recordings may reveal hesitation, attention, confusion, or emphasis that a transcript omits. If time permits, listen or watch while taking selective notes. If using transcript search, inspect the surrounding exchange rather than treating a keyword hit as a finding.
11. **Allow synthesis to be a distinct thinking stage.** Raw notes do not need to look final while evidence is being gathered. A reusable evidence map should organize them before a task-specific copy plan is made.
12. **Do not treat old case results as forecasts.** Historical outcomes show that a method produced a useful hypothesis in a particular setting. They are not universal benchmarks.

## Workflow

1. **Frame the decision.** State what the research must help decide, which audience is in scope, the action being optimized, and what would count as usable evidence.
2. **Map known and missing evidence.** Inventory customer access, existing qualitative material, behavioral data, product facts, competitor material, and operational facts. Mark assumptions explicitly.
3. **Select a mixed method plan.** Pair at least one source of language or motive with one independent source when feasible. Recruit within the target segment and plan for non-response.
4. **Prepare neutral instruments.** Draft open prompts around past behavior and decision episodes. Add probes for triggers, alternatives, objections, anxieties, desired outcomes, consequences, influences, and results. Have another reviewer check for leading wording.
5. **Run interviews and recording reviews actively.** Obtain permission, record when allowed, and keep a backup where appropriate. Take enough notes to flag meaningful wording, delivery signals, and follow-ups. Recap the meaning heard and invite correction. Use short probes to move from abstractions to events. End with an open invitation for anything missed.
6. **Mine reviews and public discussions.** Define the audience, desired outcome, current alternatives, and likely venues first. For rapid discovery, use constrained searches and language markers to locate candidate passages, then inspect the complete source. Capture exact excerpts with source, date, rating or context, segment clues, and the outcome or concern represented.
7. **Collect quantitative and behavioral context.** Record acquisition source, page goal, baseline behavior, conversion definition, click concentration, and scroll reach. Do not assign a reason to observed behavior without qualitative support.
8. **Capture first, then normalize.** Keep rough notes and raw evidence recoverable. On review, store each item with provenance and classify it under trigger, prior state, attempted solution, alternative, desired progress, objection, anxiety, proof need, result, and vocabulary. Distinguish direct quotations from summaries and inference.
9. **Build a reusable evidence map.** Organize validated product facts, audience or segment evidence, motivators, switch forces, failed solutions, desired outcomes, beliefs, purchase criteria, decision constraints, competitor sentiment, inaction costs, proof needs, offer considerations, and past experiments. Update this map as evidence changes.
10. **Synthesize.** Identify cross-source themes, disagreements, segment differences, missing answers, and distinctive phrases. Build a proposed message hierarchy based on relevance, frequency, decision impact, and strength of evidence.
11. **Create the task-specific decision brief.** Pull only the evidence needed for the asset. State the one reader, offer, primary job, control-page issues when relevant, core problem, proposed remedy, proof, and action before selecting a copy framework.
12. **Convert findings into hypotheses.** For each proposed message, specify audience, placement, supporting evidence, expected effect, risk, and validation method. Flag product or operational questions exposed by the research.

## Controlled Failures

- **No direct customer access:** use adjacent reviews, forums, support archives, or competitor-customer material, label the evidence as indirect, and reduce confidence.
- **Only search snippets are available:** use them to identify candidate sources, not as quotable or decision-ready evidence. If the full context cannot be inspected, mark the finding blocked.
- **Low interview turnout:** recruit more people than the minimum needed, extend the collection window, and report the final sample rather than hiding attrition.
- **Too few observations to see patterns:** publish provisional themes and unanswered questions; do not manufacture consensus or claim saturation.
- **Leading instrument discovered after collection:** quarantine affected answers, revise the instrument, and rerun the critical questions where possible.
- **Recording fails:** rely on contemporaneous notes only if their limitations are explicit; do not reconstruct quotations from memory.
- **Transcript and observed delivery diverge:** retain both, describe the nonverbal signal as an observation rather than a motive, and seek corroboration.
- **Sample does not match the intended audience:** segment or discard mismatched evidence rather than blending it into the target profile.
- **Sources conflict:** preserve the conflict, investigate whether segment, timing, acquisition source, or use case explains it, and test competing hypotheses.
- **Sensitive or identifying material appears:** redact, aggregate, or omit it according to consent and privacy limits. Never turn private disclosures into public copy by default.
- **Behavioral data lacks context or is stale:** use it as a weak directional signal and request current, correctly instrumented evidence.
- **A striking quotation is unrepresentative:** retain it as an outlier or test candidate, not as the primary message without corroboration.
- **The evidence map becomes unwieldy:** keep raw material and history separate from the concise, current map used for decisions.

## Validation

Before handing research to a writer or agent, verify that:

- Every finding can be traced to a full source and is clearly distinguished from interpretation.
- The audience, timeframe, sample, method, and major limitations are stated.
- Interview and survey prompts passed a neutrality review.
- Search and transcript hits were checked in their surrounding context.
- Motive claims have qualitative support; behavioral claims have behavioral support; delivery signals remain observations unless corroborated.
- Priority themes are supported by more than one observation and, where practical, more than one method.
- Segment differences and contradictory evidence remain visible.
- Quotations are exact in the research record, permission-safe, and not silently polished.
- Product, price, commitment, use-case, and delivery facts are current and operationally verified.
- The evidence map covers the trigger, desired progress, alternatives, failed solutions, objections, constraints, proof needs, and next decision.
- The task-specific brief can be traced back to the reusable evidence map.
- Historical conversion figures are not presented as expected or general results.
- Proposed copy is framed as a testable hypothesis with a baseline and success measure.
