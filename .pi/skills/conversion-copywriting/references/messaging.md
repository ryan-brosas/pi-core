# Messaging Reference

## Job

Create and maintain a decision-ready messaging system that tells writers and agents whom the brand serves, what valuable progress it enables, why it is a credible choice among real alternatives, which messages take priority, and how those messages should sound and appear across assets. The system should support copy hypotheses, page hierarchy, controlled variants, implementation, validation, and learning rather than act as a static brand statement.

## Inputs

- Synthesized voice-of-customer research with provenance and limitations
- Audience segments, buying context, decision style, and awareness level
- Product capabilities, delivery model, pricing, guarantees, and operational constraints
- Customer outcomes, objections, testimonials, reviews, case studies, credentials, and approved data
- Direct competitors, indirect alternatives, and the option to do nothing
- Existing positioning, mission, values, brand voice, and style rules
- Current website, campaigns, bios, content, sales materials, and rendered controls
- Search intent, funnel stages, channel constraints, page goals, and interaction constraints
- Baselines, experiments, prior validations, and stakeholder ownership
- Design and implementation partners who control how message hierarchy reaches the audience

## Decision Rules

1. **Start with customer and market evidence.** Internal preference can shape ambition and voice, but it cannot establish what customers value or which alternatives they consider.
2. **Require synthesis before drafting.** Research is not strategy until evidence has been organized into a hierarchy and a testable message hypothesis. Do not jump from raw notes to polished copy.
3. **Treat positioning, value proposition, and unique selling proposition as related but distinct tools.** Positioning defines the market context, audience, category, and differentiated value. A value proposition states the value promised. A USP adds a meaningful distinction that competitors cannot truthfully or easily match. Use the terms consistently inside the project even if source traditions differ.
4. **Name the real alternatives.** Competition may include another vendor, an internal workaround, delay, manual work, or no action. Position against what buyers actually consider.
5. **Choose a narrow value that matters greatly to a defined segment.** A broad claim for everyone is weaker than a credible advantage for the people most likely to care.
6. **Distinguish category requirements from differentiation.** Required features establish legitimacy. They are not the lead message unless research shows the market doubts them.
7. **Make differentiation operationally true.** A quantified promise, deadline, guarantee, or comparative claim requires product, service, and legal support. If the business cannot deliver it, do not write it.
8. **Prefer concrete consequences to summary benefits.** Generic references to convenience, savings, quality, or innovation must be unpacked into the situation, result, and significance for this audience.
9. **Prioritize the dominant value rather than combining incompatible appeals.** Research whether the audience chiefly protects time, cash, status, identity, control, safety, or another scarce resource. Do not assume that two broad benefits are equally persuasive.
10. **Attach proof to the claim it supports.** A testimonial, result, credential, review, demonstration, or guarantee should answer a specific doubt. Proof volume does not compensate for irrelevance.
11. **Match expression to awareness.** Ready buyers need a direct offer and path to act. Product-aware buyers need fit and comparison. Solution-aware buyers need the product connected to the desired outcome. Problem-aware buyers need recognition of the situation before a solution-heavy pitch. Unaware audiences usually require disproportionate education and budget.
12. **Keep one reader, one governing idea, one promise, and one offer in view for a focused asset.** A persona summarizes recurring behavior, context, priorities, and decision needs; invented biography should not drive copy.
13. **Use values and story only when they clarify buyer value or earned credibility.** A brand story should explain the problem taken on, the approach, and the resulting customer benefit, not turn the customer into an audience for founder mythology.
14. **Voice serves comprehension and trust.** It may sound human and distinctive, but it must not obscure claims, imitate customer speech unnaturally, or conflict with the seriousness of the decision.
15. **Message hierarchy includes presentation.** Wireframes, layout, visual emphasis, and interaction order can change what readers notice and believe. Writers should specify the intended sequence and remain involved through implementation.
16. **Use the full conversion cycle.** Research and discovery, writing and presentation, and validation and experimentation are all required responsibilities, although their relative effort changes by project.
17. **Treat the strategy as versioned and revisable.** New research, product changes, market shifts, implementation evidence, and test results can change the hierarchy.

## Workflow

1. **Define scope and vocabulary.** Name the segment, buying situation, offer, channel, conversion goal, governing idea, promise, and primary action. Record the project's definitions of positioning, value proposition, USP, proof, and voice.
2. **Assemble and synthesize the evidence.** Bring together customer language, decision episodes, alternatives, objections, outcomes, behavioral data, validated product facts, and stakeholder context. Mark confidence and gaps, then form a research question or message hypothesis before drafting.
3. **Map the competitive frame.** List direct and indirect alternatives. Capture how each presents its category, audience, lead value, proof, and trade-offs. Do not copy their language.
4. **Answer the positioning questions.** Determine what buyers would do without the offer, which capabilities are meaningfully different, what value those capabilities produce, who cares most, and which context makes that value obvious.
5. **Draft positioning hypotheses.** Write a plain descriptor containing audience, category, and principal value. Then explore a more memorable form only if it remains clear, true, and exclusive enough to be useful.
6. **Draft the value proposition.** State the customer's relevant situation, the promised progress, and why this approach is better suited than the alternatives. Remove jargon and unsupported superiority.
7. **Decide whether a USP exists.** Look for a defensible capability, method, asset, constraint, result, or risk reversal. If none exists, keep a strong value proposition and flag a product or operations opportunity rather than inventing uniqueness.
8. **Build the message hierarchy.** Rank the lead message, supporting benefits, mechanism, proof, objection handling, risk reversal, and action. Map each item to evidence and awareness level.
9. **Document the audience lens.** Summarize observed context, trigger, desired progress, hesitations, alternatives, information needs, and likely decision pace. Exclude decorative demographics unless they affect the decision.
10. **Document values and promise.** Keep values specific, observable, and actionable. State the conditions and limits of any customer-facing promise.
11. **Define voice and style.** Specify a small set of voice traits, desired reader effect, words to use or avoid, grammar and punctuation conventions, and original examples of in-bounds and out-of-bounds expression created for this brand.
12. **Turn the system into copy and presentation.** Select evidence-backed customer language as raw material, revise it into coherent brand copy, and map the message sequence in a wireframe or equivalent hierarchy before visual design. Do not hand raw quotations or disconnected sections to implementation.
13. **Create controlled variants.** Adapt the core strategy into a short descriptor, homepage proposition, campaign angle, bio, comparison message, content themes, and segment-specific versions without changing the underlying claim. Record the hypothesis behind each meaningful variant.
14. **Map content to awareness.** Prioritize comparison and alternative content for product-aware demand, capability and use-case content for solution-aware demand, and educational or research-led content for problem-aware demand. Make a direct action available wherever ready buyers may arrive.
15. **Review implementation.** Confirm that design, responsive layout, interaction, and production copy preserve the intended hierarchy, proof proximity, and action. Resolve changes with designers and developers rather than treating the copy document as the final customer experience.
16. **Validate and experiment.** Use brief-exposure or comprehension checks for clarity and recall, moderated research for meaning and friction, behavioral evidence for actual use, and controlled experiments for causal claims when traffic permits. Do not ask a method to prove what it cannot measure.
17. **Assign governance.** Record owner, version, evidence date, approved claims, prohibited claims, implementation notes, review cadence, and test history.

## Controlled Failures

- **Raw research exists but no hierarchy emerges:** pause drafting, identify the unresolved decision, and synthesize a provisional hierarchy with explicit gaps.
- **No credible difference emerges:** use the clearest relevant value proposition available, narrow the segment, or recommend a product or service change. Never fabricate exclusivity.
- **A proposed claim exceeds delivery capability:** weaken or remove it, or route it to operations for validation before publication.
- **Competitors can make the same statement:** deepen the mechanism, context, segment, proof, or trade-off. If the statement remains category-generic, demote it.
- **Customer evidence conflicts with founder preference:** show both, state the business trade-off, and test bounded alternatives rather than blending them into vague copy.
- **Several segments want different outcomes:** create a shared parent position only if it is true and useful; otherwise segment the message and route traffic deliberately.
- **Proof is missing:** mark the claim unverified, use a modest factual statement, and create an evidence collection plan.
- **A quantified or comparative claim may create legal risk:** require source documentation and appropriate review; do not publish by inference.
- **Voice makes the message less clear or credible:** keep the strategic claim and simplify the expression.
- **A persona contains invented traits:** remove any detail without evidence or decision relevance.
- **A clarity check is mistaken for persuasion evidence:** restrict the conclusion to comprehension or recall and use a suitable method for persuasion or conversion.
- **A/B testing is unavailable or inappropriate:** use audience review, comprehension testing, rendered-page observation, and post-launch behavior. Keep causal claims provisional.
- **Implementation changes the hierarchy:** review the rendered experience with design and development, then restore the intended sequence or document and validate the new treatment.
- **The strategy becomes a large archive no one can use:** separate the concise approved system from supporting research and history.
- **Old campaign results are offered as expected outcomes:** retain them only as dated case evidence with context, never as a general forecast.

## Validation

Approve the messaging system only when:

- A new writer can identify the audience, category, primary value, alternatives, difference, proof, governing idea, promise, offer, and next action without interpretation from a stakeholder.
- Every material claim maps to current evidence or an operational fact.
- The hierarchy is a documented synthesis rather than a collection of raw notes.
- The lead value is important to the chosen segment and not merely a category baseline.
- The differentiation passes a competitor truth test and a delivery test.
- The wording is understandable on first read and avoids vague business language.
- Proof is adjacent and relevant to the claim it supports.
- Awareness-specific variants preserve the same strategic truth.
- Comparison content is accurate, current, non-deceptive, and properly qualified.
- Values, promise, story, and voice reinforce rather than compete with customer value.
- The wireframe or rendered experience preserves message order, proof proximity, and the primary action across relevant breakpoints.
- Validation conclusions match the capability of the method used. Brief exposure establishes clarity or recall, not persuasion or conversion.
- Test plans define audience, baseline, complete treatment, primary metric, guardrails, and decision rule.
- No historical conversion figure has been generalized into a promise or benchmark.
- The document has an owner, version, evidence date, implementation record, and scheduled review.
