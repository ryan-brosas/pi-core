# About Page Copywriting

Write an About page that helps one reader decide whether to trust the person or team. Do not write a company timeline, a long resume, or a homepage in disguise.

This skill independently adapts useful decisions from these Copyhackers resources.

- [12 tips to write an About Us page that builds trust](https://copyhackers.com/2022/08/how-to-write-about-us-page/)
- [Is your About page embarrassing? Here are 7 mistakes you may be making](https://copyhackers.com/2015/03/write-an-about-page/)
- [7 copywriting research techniques conversion copywriters love](https://copyhackers.com/2022/06/copywriting-research/)

Use the decisions. Do not copy the source voice, jokes, examples, templates, or phrases.

## Hard Copy Rules

Apply these rules to all visible copy, headings, titles, and metadata.

- Aim for Flesch Kincaid grade 6. Treat grade 6 as the ceiling.
- Never use an em dash.
- Never use a colon.
- Keep the page focused on one reader and the trust they need.
- Use only checked facts and approved public evidence.
- Do not invent clients, praise, pain, years, scale, revenue, motives, or impact.
- Do not use a logo, quote, photo, name, or private detail without permission.
- Write in the owner's real point of view. Do not use "we" for solo work.
- End with one primary next step.

## Find the One Reader

An About page is about the business only when those facts help the reader trust it.

Define one reader from the overlap of the best-fit audience. Record these points.

- What are they trying to do?
- What makes them unsure?
- What do they need to trust before they act?
- What proof can answer that doubt?
- What should they do after the page?

Do not fill the page with facts just because they are true. Keep a fact only when it helps this reader understand fit, skill, judgment, or trust.

## Research Before Writing

Read the target site's current About page, home page, service copy, case studies, proof records, analytics notes, and content rules before asking for facts already present.

Use more than one source when available.

- Interviews show how clients or readers describe the choice.
- Reviews show natural words for needs, doubts, and outcomes.
- Short polls show what brought a reader to the site.
- Analytics show how people reach and use the page.
- Heatmaps show what they click and how far they read.
- Competitor review shows repeated claims and open space. Learn from it without copying.

For a new portfolio with little audience data, use approved work evidence, service inquiries, search terms, and case-study facts. Mark all assumptions as unknown. Do not turn the owner's self-view into voice-of-customer proof.

## Build the About Packet

Gather and sort the material before drafting.

- One reader
- Main trust question
- Reader goal and current problem
- What the person or team helps with
- Why that work matters to them
- Relevant experience and proof
- Working principles shown in action
- Short origin detail when it explains a useful choice
- Real personality details that fit the work
- Approved photo or visual evidence
- Claim-to-proof pairs
- One primary CTA and one lower-pressure fallback when needed

Tag each item as fact, inference, unknown, or blocked. Remove blocked material from public copy.

## Choose What Belongs

Use three filters for every section.

1. Does it matter to the one reader?
2. Does it fit this kind of business or portfolio?
3. Can current experience and evidence support it?

Cut any section that fails the trust test. A mission, value, timeline, credential, process, or personal detail has no automatic right to appear.

## Shape the Page

Use this order as a starting point. Change it only when research supports a better order.

### Reader opening

Start with the reader's goal, doubt, or reason for checking the page. Show that they are in the right place. Do not open with a birth year, job title, or broad claim about passion.

### Fit and help

State who the work is for, what kind of problem it handles, and what the reader can expect. Keep this narrower than the home page. The goal is trust, not a full sales pitch.

### Bridge to the person or team

Move from the reader's need to the reason this person or team works this way. Make the link clear. Do not switch into a detached biography with no tie back to the reader.

### Relevant story

Share only the parts of the origin, experience, or point of view that explain present skill and judgment. Use a real moment, choice, or lesson when one is supported. Avoid a full career timeline.

### How the work is done

Show principles through actions. Instead of saying the team values care, show the check, review, limit, or choice that proves care.

### Proof near the claim

Place each metric, case study, credential, quote, or logo next to the claim it supports. Random proof bars do not build clear trust. If proof is missing or permission is unclear, remove the claim or mark the work as still in development.

### Human signal

Use a real photo, small work detail, or plain personal note when it helps the reader see who is behind the work. Use approved assets and useful alt text. Do not use a stock image as proof of personality.

### Clear close

Give the reader one logical next step. Match it to their stage and the trust built on the page. A portfolio may lead to case studies, services, or a short inquiry. Do not leave the reader to guess.

## Make Crossheads Carry the Story

Read only the title and section headings in order. They should still tell a clear story from reader need to fit, proof, person, and next step.

Rewrite labels such as "Our Mission," "Our Values," and "Meet the Team" when they say nothing useful to a skim reader.

## Keep the Bio Relevant

A useful bio answers one question.

> What does this reader need to know about this person to trust them with this problem?

Prefer proof of judgment, work, and limits. Cut old roles, awards, hobbies, and life facts that do not help the decision. Keep one human detail only when it makes the person easier to know without taking over the page.

## Avoid Common About Page Failures

Remove these patterns.

- Ego-first openings and long lists of credentials
- A full company history with no link to reader value
- Empty mission and value claims
- Claims about passion, excellence, or leadership with no proof
- Marketing terms that could fit any competitor
- Long copy that repeats the home or service page
- Random testimonials and logo walls
- A faceless page when an approved real image is available
- Several equal CTAs or no CTA at all

## Keep Grade 6 Copy Natural

- Put one main thought in each sentence.
- Aim for 8 to 16 words in most sentences.
- Use common words and clear verbs.
- Name the person who does the work.
- Explain a needed technical term in the same line.
- Use short sections and useful lists.
- Keep a natural flow. Do not make every line short just to lower the score.

Never remove a proof limit or permission note to lower the grade. Rewrite it in plain words.

## Remove AI Slop

Delete or rewrite these patterns.

- Empty openings about a fast-paced world
- Stock words such as delve, unlock, seamless, robust, transformative, revolutionary, cutting-edge, game-changer, landscape, journey, and testament
- Claims about being passionate, world-class, visionary, or driven by excellence
- Staged lines built as "not just X, but Y"
- Forced groups of three that add no meaning
- Vague lines about being at an intersection or pushing boundaries
- Generic founder stories about a lifelong journey
- Fake warmth, fake humility, and claims that tell the reader what to feel
- Repeated summary lines and canned future endings

Prefer a real detail, a checked result, a clear choice, and a plain limit.

## Quality Gate

Run the checker from this skill directory against the final Markdown or plain text.

```bash
node scripts/check-copy.mjs path/to/draft.md
```

Resolve every grade, em dash, colon, and stock-phrase failure. Then review claims, permissions, message order, and CTA fit by hand.

Report these facts before completion.

- Estimated reading grade
- Em dash count of zero
- Colon count of zero
- Unsupported claim count of zero
- Permission gaps of zero
- One-reader trust question
- Primary CTA
- Remaining evidence limits

Do not claim completion when the checker fails, the page is still ego-first, a claim lacks proof, or publication permission is unclear.
